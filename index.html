Categorical Variables, ANOVA & Interactions

▼

Categorical (Dummy) Variables

  • A categorical predictor with $G$ levels is encoded as $G - 1$ dummy (indicator) variables. The omitted level is the reference category.
  • Each slope $\hat\beta_j$ represents the estimated difference in expected $y$ between category $j$ and the reference category.
  • Cannot use $G$ dummies + intercept: the $G$ dummies sum to the intercept column $\Rightarrow$ perfect collinearity, $X^\top X$ non-invertible.
$$\hat{y}_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \mathbf{1}\{x_i = c_1\} + \cdots + \hat\beta_{G-1}\mathbf{1}\{x_i = c_{G-1}\}$$

Predictions by group: Reference → $\hat\beta_0$; Category $j$ → $\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_j$.

ANOVA via the Overall F-Test

Regressing $y$ on a single categorical predictor and using the overall F-test is equivalent to a one-way Analysis of Variance.

$$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \cdots = \mu_G \qquad H_a: \text{at least one } \mu_g \text{ differs}$$

Assumes homoskedasticity ($\sigma_1 = \cdots = \sigma_G$) and normality under the stronger linear model.

Combining Continuous & Categorical Predictors

Including both adjusts the categorical slopes: they now represent differences holding continuous variables fixed.

Interaction Terms

Allow slopes to differ across groups. For continuous $\times$ categorical:

$$x_i \cdot \mathbf{1}\{x_j = c\} = \begin{cases} x_i & \text{if } x_j = c \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Each group gets its own intercept and slope. Including all interactions is equivalent to fitting separate regressions per group.

HW 4 — Problem 1(d): Separate Prediction Equations
Given the regression Income ~ Education * Millenial with output:
Intercept = 21.13, Education = 0.399, MillenialTRUE = −8.153, Education:MillenialTRUE = −0.086.
Provide separate prediction equations for Millenials and Non-Millenials.

Non-Millenial: $\widehat{\text{Income}} = 21.13 + 0.399 \cdot \text{Education}$

Millenial: $\widehat{\text{Income}} = (21.13 - 8.15) + (0.399 - 0.086)\cdot \text{Education} = 12.98 + 0.313 \cdot \text{Education}$

HW 4 — Problem 1(j): Simpson's Paradox
Analysis I (simple regression of Income on Education) shows a negative slope. Analysis II (with Millenial interaction) shows a positive slope on Education. Explain the discrepancy.

This is Simpson's Paradox. The overall trend (aggregated) is not the same as the within-group trends. Millenials tend to have more education but lower income. Once we separate groups, the relationship between education and income within each group is positive, but the confounding effect of the Millenial variable reverses the sign when aggregated.

Also, slopes in the two models don't have the same interpretation — different variables are being held fixed.

Partial F-Test & Continuous Interactions

▼

The Partial F-Test

Compares a full model (all predictors) to a reduced model (subset). Tests whether a group of coefficients are jointly zero.

$$F = \frac{(\text{RSS}_{\text{red}} - \text{RSS}_{\text{full}}) / (df_{\text{red}} - df_{\text{full}})}{\text{RSS}_{\text{full}} / df_{\text{full}}} \sim F_{df_{\text{red}} - df_{\text{full}},\; df_{\text{full}}}$$
  • $H_0$: $\beta_j = 0$ for all $j$ in subset $\mathcal{I}$; $H_a$: at least one $\beta_j \neq 0$
  • Numerator df = $p_{\text{full}} - p_{\text{red}}$ (number of extra predictors)
  • Denominator df = $n - p_{\text{full}} - 1$

Overall F-Test (Special Case)

Reduced model = intercept only → $\text{RSS}_{\text{red}} = \text{TSS}$, $df_{\text{red}} = n-1$.

$$F = \frac{R^2 / p}{(1 - R^2)/(n - p - 1)}$$

$t^2 = F_{1,df}$ Relationship

A partial F-test comparing models that differ by exactly one predictor is equivalent to the t-test on that predictor. If $T \sim t_{df}$, then $T^2 \sim F_{1,df}$. Same p-value.

Continuous Interactions

For two continuous predictors $X_1, X_2$, include $X_1 \cdot X_2$ as a predictor. The effect of $X_1$ on $y$ now depends on the value of $X_2$.

$$\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial x_1} = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_{12} \cdot x_2$$

Linear Combinations of Slopes

To get the slope on a predictor in a particular group (with interactions), define $\mathbf{a}$ and compute $\mathbf{a}^\top \hat\beta$.

$$\text{Var}(\mathbf{a}^\top \hat\beta) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \;\mathbf{a}^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \mathbf{a}$$ $$\text{se}(\mathbf{a}^\top \hat\beta) = \hat\sigma_\varepsilon \sqrt{\mathbf{a}^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \mathbf{a}}$$

Cannot compute from summary() alone — need $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon$ and the design matrix $X$.

Practice Midterm 2 — Problem 2(b): Partial F-Test

Conditional Expectations & Prediction Intervals

▼

Inference for Conditional Expectations

Estimate $\mu_{y|\tilde{x}} = E(y \mid x = \tilde{x}) = \tilde{x}^\top \beta$ with $\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}} = \tilde{x}^\top \hat\beta$.

$$\text{Var}(\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \;\tilde{x}^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \tilde{x}$$

95% Confidence Interval

$$\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}} \;\pm\; t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-p-1} \cdot \hat\sigma_\varepsilon \sqrt{\tilde{x}^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \tilde{x}}$$

Variance is smallest when $\tilde{x}$ is at the mean of predictors; increases as $\tilde{x}$ moves away.

Prediction Intervals

For a future observation $y^* = \tilde{x}^\top \beta + \varepsilon^*$, we need to account for two sources of uncertainty:

  • Estimation uncertainty: $\text{Var}(\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}})$
  • Inherent variability: $\text{Var}(\varepsilon^*) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2$
$$\text{Var}(y^* - \hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \left(1 + \tilde{x}^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \tilde{x}\right)$$

Prediction Interval Formula

$$\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}} \;\pm\; t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-p-1} \cdot \hat\sigma_\varepsilon \sqrt{1 + \tilde{x}^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \tilde{x}}$$

PI vs CI: Key Differences

Confidence IntervalPrediction Interval
Target$\mu_{y|\tilde{x}} = E(y|\tilde{x})$ (population mean)$y^*$ (individual future obs.)
Width factor$\sqrt{\tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}}$$\sqrt{1 + \tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}}$
Wider?NarrowerAlways wider
As $n \to \infty$Width → 0Width → $2 z_{1-\alpha/2} \sigma_\varepsilon$

Assumptions needed: Linearity provides the center; homoskedasticity and normality provide the width.

$$7.49 \pm 1.96 \times 0.31 \times \sqrt{1 + 0.0060} = 7.49 \pm 0.6098$$

Regression Diagnostics: Linearity & Homoskedasticity

▼

Stronger Linear Model Assumptions

  1. Linearity: $E(y|x_i) = x_i^\top \beta$
  2. Homoskedasticity: $\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i | x_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2$ for all $i$
  3. Normality: $\varepsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2)$

The Hat Matrix

$$H = X(X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \qquad \hat{y} = Hy \qquad e = (I - H)y$$

Properties (with intercept, $p$ predictors)

  • $\frac{1}{n} \le h_{ii} \le 1$ and $\sum_i h_{ii} = p + 1$
  • $\text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1 - h_{ii})$, $\text{Var}(\hat{y}_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 h_{ii}$
  • $\text{Cov}(e_i, \hat{y}_j) = 0$ for all $i,j$ (under the weaker model)

Standardized (Internally Studentized) Residuals

Raw residuals $e_i$ don't have equal variance. Fix:

$$r_i = \frac{e_i}{\hat\sigma_\varepsilon \sqrt{1 - h_{ii}}}$$

Under the stronger model, $\text{Var}(r_i) \approx 1$ (not exactly, since $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon$ is estimated).

Diagnostic Plots

Assessing Linearity

  • Plot $e_i$ vs. $\hat{y}_i$ or $e_i$ vs. each $x_{ij}$
  • Good: amorphous blob, no pattern
  • Bad: curvature, systematic positive/negative patches

Assessing Homoskedasticity

  • Plot $r_i$ or $\sqrt{|r_i|}$ vs. $\hat{y}_i$ or $x_{ij}$
  • Good: constant spread
  • Bad: "fanning out" (spread increases with fitted values)
HW 6 — Problem 2(b): When PI Formula Breaks
Under a model where $y_i = x_i^\top\beta + \varepsilon_i \cdot \sqrt{\sum x_{ij}^2}$ (heteroskedastic errors), would you trust the standard 80% PI formula?

No. The standard PI formula assumes homoskedasticity — it uses a single $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon$ pooled across all observations. Here, the variance of $y^*$ depends on $\tilde{x}$, so the width of the interval should vary with $\tilde{x}$. The standard formula would produce intervals that are too wide for some $\tilde{x}$ and too narrow for others.

Assessing Normality & Leverage

▼

Normal QQ-Plots

Assess normality of $\varepsilon_i$ using standardized residuals $r_i$.

How to Read

  • Points on diagonal: approximately normal
  • Convex (cup) curve: right-skewed
  • Concave curve: left-skewed
  • S-shape / heavy tails: points too high on right, too low on left

Use standardized residuals (not raw) because they are identically distributed under the stronger model.

Leverage ($h_{ii}$)

Leverage measures the potential for a point to influence the regression line. It depends only on predictors.

$$h_{ii} = x_i^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_i \qquad \hat{y}_i = h_{ii} y_i + \sum_{j \neq i} h_{ij} y_j$$
  • If $h_{ii} = 1$: $\hat{y}_i = y_i$, $e_i = 0$ — the point perfectly fits itself
  • High leverage = point is far from the mean of predictors ("outlier in $X$")
  • High leverage alone doesn't mean the point is influencing the regression — it has the potential to do so
$$\text{Rule of thumb: } h_{ii} > \frac{2(p+1)}{n} \text{ is high leverage}$$
Practice Midterm 2 — Problem 4(b): Leverage of Unique Category
What would you expect the leverage value to be for a song with a unique value of artist.terms (a categorical predictor with 299 levels)?

$h_{ii} = 1$. Since this category has only one observation, the model has a dedicated coefficient that is set so $\hat{y}_i = y_i$ exactly. The point perfectly predicts itself.

HW 6 — Problem 1(c): Identifying High-Leverage Points
For a fuel usage regression with 51 observations and 4 predictors, identify the highest-leverage point and explain why it has high leverage.

The threshold is $2(4+1)/51 = 0.196$. The highest-leverage point has predictor values far from the means of the other observations. You identify it from the histogram of leverages or the residuals-vs-leverage plot, then compare its predictor values to the dataset's means.

Detecting Outliers & Influential Points

▼

Externally Studentized Residuals

To test for outliers, exclude point $i$, refit, and check if $y_i$ is far from its new prediction $\hat{y}_{(i)}$.

$$t_i = \frac{y_i - \hat{y}_{(i)}}{\hat\sigma_{\varepsilon(i)} \sqrt{1 + x_i^\top(X_{(i)}^\top X_{(i)})^{-1} x_i}} \sim t_{n-p-2}$$

Shortcut (no need to refit!):

$$t_i = r_i \sqrt{\frac{n - (p+1) - 1}{n - (p+1) - r_i^2}}$$

where $r_i$ is the internally studentized residual.

Testing for Outliers

  • For a single observation: reject if $|t_i| > t_{1-\alpha/2,\; n-p-2}$
  • For all $n$ observations: use Bonferroni correction — test each at level $\alpha/n$

Influential Points & Cook's Distance

A point is influential if removing it substantially changes the fitted values.

$$D_i = \frac{1}{p+1} \cdot r_i^2 \cdot \frac{h_{ii}}{1 - h_{ii}}$$
  • Combines large residual ($r_i^2$) and high leverage ($h_{ii}$)
  • Rule of thumb: $D_i > 1$ suggests an influential point
  • High leverage but small residual → not influential
  • Large residual but low leverage → usually not very influential

What to Do with Outliers

  • Good reasons to remove: data entry error, individual shouldn't be in sample
  • Bad reasons: "slopes become more significant," "R² increases"
  • Outliers can indicate the model doesn't apply to the full population, or a lurking variable is needed
Practice Midterm 2 — Problem 3(b): High Leverage Assessment
With 366 data points and 7 predictors, do any observations have high leverage? Are you concerned?

Threshold: $2(7+1)/366 = 0.044$. Yes, some points have leverage > 0.044. However, no high-leverage points are paired with large standardized residuals, so they are not influential (they follow the trend). No concern.

HW 6 — Problem 1(d): Bonferroni Outlier Testing
Test whether any of the 51 data points are outliers, controlling the familywise Type I error rate at 0.05.

Compute externally studentized residuals $t_i$ for all 51 points. Compare each $|t_i|$ to $t_{1 - 0.05/(2 \cdot 51),\; 51-4-2} = t_{0.99951, 45}$. Equivalently, compute each p-value as $2P(T > |t_i|)$ and compare to $0.05/51 \approx 0.00098$. Only flag as outliers those that exceed this threshold.

Multicollinearity & Multiple Hypothesis Tests

▼

Multicollinearity

Two or more predictors are strongly linearly related. Makes $X^\top X$ nearly singular.

Statistical Consequences

  • Imprecise / unstable $\hat\beta$ — small changes in data → big changes in estimates
  • t-tests fail to detect significant predictors (inflated standard errors)
  • Coefficients can have "weird" signs
  • Hallmark: significant overall F-test but no individual t-tests are significant

Diagnosing Multicollinearity

1. Pairwise Correlations

Check for correlations > 0.9. Limitation: won't catch one predictor as a combination of many.

2. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)

$$\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

where $R_j^2$ = R² from regressing $x_j$ on all other predictors. Rule of thumb: VIF > 10 is concerning (some use 5).

3. Condition Number

$$\kappa = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_p}}$$

where $\lambda_1, \lambda_p$ = largest and smallest eigenvalues of the predictor correlation matrix.

Rules of thumb: $\kappa > 15$ moderate; $\kappa > 30$ severe.

VIF ↔ R² Relationship

From HW6: given $\text{VIF}_j$, you can back out the $R^2$ of regressing $x_j$ on the other predictors:

$$R_j^2 = 1 - \frac{1}{\text{VIF}_j}$$

Partial Regression

  1. Regress $y$ on all predictors except $x_j$ → residuals $y_R$
  2. Regress $x_j$ on all other predictors → residuals $x_{j,R}$
  3. Simple regression of $y_R$ on $x_{j,R}$: slope = $\hat\beta_j$ from the full model

Partial regression plot ($y_R$ vs $x_{j,R}$) can detect nonlinearity and influential points for that predictor.

Multiple Hypothesis Tests

With $K$ tests at level $\alpha$: $P(\text{at least one Type I error}) \le K\alpha$ (can be as high as $1 - (1-\alpha)^K$).

Bonferroni Correction

$$\text{Test each hypothesis at level } \frac{\alpha}{K} \implies \text{FWER} \le \alpha$$

Holm-Bonferroni (Uniformly Better)

Order p-values $p_1 \le p_2 \le \cdots \le p_K$:

  • Step $i$: Is $p_i \le \alpha / (K + 1 - i)$? If yes, reject and continue. If no, stop.
  • Rejects at least as many hypotheses as Bonferroni.
Practice Midterm 2 — Problem 3(a): VIF & Condition Number
All VIFs are near 1 (max 1.25) and the condition number is 1.76. Is there multicollinearity?

No. All VIFs are well below 5 (or 10), and the condition number is well below 15. Neither metric raises any concerns about multicollinearity.

Practice Midterm 2 — Problem 4(a): Too Many Categories
A categorical predictor has 299 levels, some with very small frequencies. What problems could this cause?

Three main issues:

  • Near-multicollinearity: highly unstable OLS coefficients
  • High variance: small-frequency categories have very imprecise coefficient estimates
  • Unidentifiability: categories appearing only in test data (not training) have no estimable coefficient

Proof Practice — Guided Derivations (Q5-Style)

▼

These multi-part problems mirror the style and difficulty of Practice Midterm 2, Problem 5. Each walks you through a proof step by step. Try each part before revealing the solution.

Problem 1: Unbiasedness of the OLS Estimator

Assume the weaker linear model: $\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with $E(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \mathbf{0}$ and $\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 I$. The OLS estimator is $\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$. We will show that $\hat\beta$ is unbiased.

Part (a) — 4 points
Substitute $\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ into the expression for $\hat\beta$ and simplify. Show that $\hat\beta = \boldsymbol{\beta} + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$.
$$\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$$ $$= (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X\boldsymbol{\beta} + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$ $$= I\boldsymbol{\beta} + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \boldsymbol{\beta} + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$
Part (b) — 3 points
Using the result from (a), take expectations of both sides. What are $E(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ and $E((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$? Conclude that $E(\hat\beta) = \boldsymbol{\beta}$.
$$E(\hat\beta) = E(\boldsymbol{\beta}) + E((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$$

$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is a fixed (non-random) vector, so $E(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \boldsymbol{\beta}$.

$(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ is a fixed matrix (it depends only on $X$, which is treated as fixed), so:

$$E((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top E(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$$

Therefore $E(\hat\beta) = \boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{0} = \boldsymbol{\beta}$. The OLS estimator is unbiased.

Part (c) — 6 points
Now derive $\text{Var}(\hat\beta)$. Using the result from (a) and the fact that $\text{Var}(A\mathbf{Z}) = A\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{Z})\,A^\top$ for a fixed matrix $A$, show that $\text{Var}(\hat\beta) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$.

From part (a): $\hat\beta = \boldsymbol{\beta} + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$. Since $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is a constant:

$$\text{Var}(\hat\beta) = \text{Var}((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$$

Let $A = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. Then:

$$\text{Var}(A\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = A\,\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\,A^\top = A(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 I)A^\top = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 AA^\top$$ $$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \left((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\right)^\top$$ $$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X ((X^\top X)^{-1})^\top$$

Since $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is symmetric: $((X^\top X)^{-1})^\top = (X^\top X)^{-1}$.

$$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2 (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top X (X^\top X)^{-1} = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}$$
Part (d) — 4 points
Identify which of the following are random and which are fixed: (i) $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, (ii) $\hat\beta$, (iii) $X$, (iv) $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, (v) $(X^\top X)^{-1}$. Briefly justify each.
  • $\boldsymbol{\beta}$: FIXED — the true (unknown) parameter vector.
  • $\hat\beta$: RANDOM — it depends on $\mathbf{y}$, which is random through $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$.
  • $X$: FIXED — the design matrix is treated as given/non-random.
  • $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$: RANDOM — the error terms are the source of randomness.
  • $(X^\top X)^{-1}$: FIXED — it is a function of $X$ only.

Problem 2: Variance of Residuals

Under the weaker linear model, we have $\mathbf{e} = (I - H)\mathbf{y}$ where $H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. We will show that $\text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1 - h_{ii})$.

Part (a) — 5 points
Using $\mathbf{e} = (I - H)\mathbf{y}$ and $\text{Var}(\mathbf{y}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 I$, show that $\text{Var}(\mathbf{e}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(I - H)$.
Hint: Use $\text{Var}(A\mathbf{Z}) = A\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{Z})\,A^\top$ and the fact that $(I - H)$ is symmetric and idempotent.
$$\text{Var}(\mathbf{e}) = \text{Var}((I - H)\mathbf{y}) = (I - H)\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{y})\,(I - H)^\top$$ $$= (I - H)(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 I)(I - H)^\top = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(I - H)(I - H)^\top$$

Since $(I - H)$ is symmetric: $(I - H)^\top = (I - H)$. So:

$$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2(I - H)(I - H)$$

Since $(I - H)$ is idempotent: $(I - H)^2 = (I - H)$. So:

$$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2(I - H)$$
Part (b) — 3 points
What is the $(i,i)$ diagonal entry of the matrix $(I - H)$? Use this to conclude that $\text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1 - h_{ii})$.

The $(i,i)$ entry of $I$ is 1, and the $(i,i)$ entry of $H$ is $h_{ii}$. So the $(i,i)$ entry of $(I - H)$ is $1 - h_{ii}$.

Since $\text{Var}(\mathbf{e}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(I - H)$, the variance of the $i$-th component is the $(i,i)$ entry:

$$\text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1 - h_{ii})$$
Part (c) — 4 points
Explain why $\text{Var}(e_i) \ne \sigma_\varepsilon^2$ in general. What does this imply about using raw residuals $e_i$ vs. standardized residuals $r_i = e_i / (\hat\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}})$ for diagnostic plots?

$\text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1 - h_{ii})$ depends on $h_{ii}$, which varies across observations. Points with higher leverage have smaller residual variance — the regression line is "pulled" closer to them, mechanically shrinking their residuals.

This means raw residuals are not identically distributed. Standardized residuals $r_i$ divide out the $(1 - h_{ii})$ factor, making them approximately identically distributed (variance $\approx 1$), which is essential for valid diagnostic plots.

Part (d) — 5 points
Show that $\text{Cov}(\mathbf{e}, \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathbf{0}$ (the zero matrix). That is, residuals and fitted values are uncorrelated under the weaker model.
Hint: Write $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = H\mathbf{y}$ and use $\text{Cov}(A\mathbf{Z}, B\mathbf{Z}) = A\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{Z})\,B^\top$.
$$\text{Cov}(\mathbf{e}, \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \text{Cov}((I - H)\mathbf{y},\; H\mathbf{y})$$ $$= (I - H)\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{y})\,H^\top = (I - H)(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 I)H^\top$$ $$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2(I - H)H$$

Since $H$ is idempotent: $(I - H)H = H - H^2 = H - H = \mathbf{0}$.

$$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$$

Problem 3: The Gauss-Markov Theorem

Under the weaker linear model, we will show that OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE). Let $\tilde\beta = C\mathbf{y}$ be any linear unbiased estimator of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, where $C$ is some fixed matrix. We write $C = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D$ for some matrix $D$.

Part (a) — 5 points
Using the fact that $\tilde\beta = C\mathbf{y}$ is unbiased (i.e., $E(\tilde\beta) = \boldsymbol{\beta}$ for all $\boldsymbol{\beta}$), show that $CX = I$. Then show that $DX = \mathbf{0}$.
Hint: Substitute $\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ and take expectations.
$$E(\tilde\beta) = E(C\mathbf{y}) = CE(\mathbf{y}) = CE(X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = CX\boldsymbol{\beta} + C\mathbf{0} = CX\boldsymbol{\beta}$$

For this to equal $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ for all $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, we need $CX = I$.

Now substitute $C = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D$:

$$CX = ((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D)X = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X + DX = I + DX$$

For $CX = I$, we need $DX = \mathbf{0}$.

Part (b) — 6 points
Compute $\text{Var}(\tilde\beta)$ using $C = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D$ and show that: $$\text{Var}(\tilde\beta) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^\top X)^{-1} + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 DD^\top$$ Hint: Use $DX = \mathbf{0}$ and the symmetry of $(X^\top X)^{-1}$.
$$\text{Var}(\tilde\beta) = \text{Var}(C\mathbf{y}) = C\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{y})\,C^\top = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 CC^\top$$

Expanding $CC^\top$:

$$CC^\top = ((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D)((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D)^\top$$ $$= (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X(X^\top X)^{-1} + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top D^\top + DX(X^\top X)^{-1} + DD^\top$$

The first term simplifies: $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X(X^\top X)^{-1} = (X^\top X)^{-1}$.

Since $DX = \mathbf{0}$: the third term is $\mathbf{0}$. The second term: $(X^\top X)^{-1}(DX)^\top = (X^\top X)^{-1}\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.

$$CC^\top = (X^\top X)^{-1} + DD^\top$$ $$\text{Var}(\tilde\beta) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^\top X)^{-1} + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 DD^\top = \text{Var}(\hat\beta) + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 DD^\top$$
Part (c) — 4 points
Explain why $\sigma_\varepsilon^2 DD^\top$ is a positive semi-definite matrix. Conclude that $\text{Var}(\tilde\beta) - \text{Var}(\hat\beta)$ is positive semi-definite, proving that OLS has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators.

For any vector $\mathbf{v}$:

$$\mathbf{v}^\top(DD^\top)\mathbf{v} = (D^\top\mathbf{v})^\top(D^\top\mathbf{v}) = \|D^\top\mathbf{v}\|^2 \ge 0$$

So $DD^\top$ is positive semi-definite (PSD), and $\sigma_\varepsilon^2 DD^\top$ is PSD.

Therefore:

$$\text{Var}(\tilde\beta) - \text{Var}(\hat\beta) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 DD^\top \succeq \mathbf{0}$$

This means for any individual coefficient $j$: $\text{Var}(\tilde\beta_j) \ge \text{Var}(\hat\beta_j)$. OLS achieves the smallest variance — it is BLUE.

Problem 4: Properties of Confidence and Prediction Intervals

Under the stronger linear model, let $\tilde{x}$ be a fixed covariate vector. The CI for $E(y|\tilde{x})$ has width proportional to $\sqrt{\tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}}$ and the PI has width proportional to $\sqrt{1 + \tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}}$.

Part (a) — 5 points
Show that $\text{Var}(\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2\,\tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}$, where $\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}} = \tilde{x}^\top\hat\beta$.
Hint: Use $\text{Var}(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{a}^\top\text{Var}(\mathbf{Z})\,\mathbf{a}$.
$$\text{Var}(\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}}) = \text{Var}(\tilde{x}^\top\hat\beta) = \tilde{x}^\top\text{Var}(\hat\beta)\,\tilde{x} = \tilde{x}^\top(\sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^\top X)^{-1})\tilde{x} = \sigma_\varepsilon^2\,\tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}$$
Part (b) — 6 points
A future observation is $y^* = \tilde{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon^*$ where $\varepsilon^*$ is independent of $\varepsilon_1, \ldots, \varepsilon_n$. Show that: $$\text{Var}(y^* - \hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1 + \tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x})$$ Hint: For independent random variables, $\text{Var}(A - B) = \text{Var}(A) + \text{Var}(B)$.

$y^*$ depends on $\varepsilon^*$ while $\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}} = \tilde{x}^\top\hat\beta$ depends on $\varepsilon_1, \ldots, \varepsilon_n$. Since $\varepsilon^*$ is independent of $\varepsilon_1, \ldots, \varepsilon_n$, $y^*$ and $\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}}$ are independent.

$$\text{Var}(y^* - \hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}}) = \text{Var}(y^*) + \text{Var}(\hat\mu_{y|\tilde{x}})$$

$\text{Var}(y^*) = \text{Var}(\tilde{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon^*) = \text{Var}(\varepsilon^*) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2$ (since $\tilde{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is fixed).

$$= \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\,\tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x} = \sigma_\varepsilon^2\left(1 + \tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}\right)$$
Part (c) — 4 points
As $n \to \infty$, the entries of $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ go to zero (assuming the predictors span the space well). What happens to the width of the CI? What happens to the width of the PI? Why can we never perfectly predict $y^*$?

As $n \to \infty$, $\tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x} \to 0$.

CI width $\propto \sqrt{\tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}} \to 0$. With infinite data, we can estimate $E(y|\tilde{x})$ perfectly.

PI width $\propto \sqrt{1 + \tilde{x}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\tilde{x}} \to \sqrt{1} = 1$, so PI width $\to 2\,z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_\varepsilon$. It never reaches zero.

Even with perfect knowledge of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, the future $y^*$ has irreducible noise $\varepsilon^*$ with variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ that no amount of data can remove.

Part (d) — 5 points
Under the heteroskedastic model $y_i = x_i^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_i \cdot g(x_i)$ where $g(x_i) > 0$ varies with $x_i$ and $\varepsilon_i \overset{iid}{\sim} N(0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2)$, would you trust the standard PI formula from lecture? Explain.

No. The standard PI formula uses a single $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon$ pooled across all observations. It assumes $\text{Var}(y_i | x_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2$ for all $i$.

Under this model, $\text{Var}(y_i | x_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 \cdot g(x_i)^2$, which varies with $x_i$. The PI width should depend on $\tilde{x}$ through $g(\tilde{x})$, but the standard formula doesn't account for this.

The result: PIs will be too wide for some $\tilde{x}$ values and too narrow for others. The claimed coverage (e.g., 95%) won't hold pointwise.

Problem 5: Hat Matrix Properties and $\text{tr}(H) = p + 1$

Let $H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ where $X$ is $n \times (p+1)$ with full column rank. We will prove several fundamental properties.

Part (a) — 4 points
Show that $H$ is idempotent: $H^2 = H$.
$$H^2 = [X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top][X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top]$$ $$= X(X^\top X)^{-1}(X^\top X)(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$$ $$= X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top = H$$
Part (b) — 4 points
Show that $H$ is symmetric: $H^\top = H$.
$$H^\top = (X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top)^\top = (X^\top)^\top ((X^\top X)^{-1})^\top X^\top = X((X^\top X)^\top)^{-1}X^\top$$

Since $X^\top X$ is symmetric: $(X^\top X)^\top = X^\top X$, so $((X^\top X)^\top)^{-1} = (X^\top X)^{-1}$.

$$H^\top = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top = H$$
Part (c) — 6 points
Show that $\text{tr}(H) = p + 1$, and therefore $\sum_{i=1}^n h_{ii} = p + 1$.
Hint: Use the cyclic property of trace: $\text{tr}(ABC) = \text{tr}(CAB)$. Note that $(X^\top X)^{-1}(X^\top X) = I_{p+1}$.
$$\text{tr}(H) = \text{tr}(X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top)$$

By the cyclic property, $\text{tr}(ABC) = \text{tr}(CAB)$. Let $A = X$, $B = (X^\top X)^{-1}$, $C = X^\top$:

$$= \text{tr}(X^\top X (X^\top X)^{-1}) = \text{tr}(I_{p+1}) = p + 1$$

Since $\text{tr}(H) = \sum_{i=1}^n h_{ii}$, we have $\sum h_{ii} = p + 1$.

Part (d) — 5 points
Using idempotency, show that $0 \le h_{ii} \le 1$ for all $i$. Also show that $h_{ii} = 1/n$ when the model has intercept only ($X = \mathbf{1}$, the column of ones).
Hint: For the bound, write $h_{ii} = \mathbf{e}_i^\top H \mathbf{e}_i = \mathbf{e}_i^\top H^2 \mathbf{e}_i = \|H\mathbf{e}_i\|^2$, and note that $\|H\mathbf{e}_i\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{e}_i\|^2 = 1$ since $H$ is a projection.

$h_{ii} = \mathbf{e}_i^\top H \mathbf{e}_i$ where $\mathbf{e}_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector. By idempotency and symmetry:

$$h_{ii} = \mathbf{e}_i^\top H^2 \mathbf{e}_i = (H\mathbf{e}_i)^\top(H\mathbf{e}_i) = \|H\mathbf{e}_i\|^2 \ge 0$$

Since $H$ is an orthogonal projection onto $\text{col}(X)$, for any vector $\mathbf{v}$: $\|H\mathbf{v}\| \le \|\mathbf{v}\|$. So $h_{ii} = \|H\mathbf{e}_i\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{e}_i\|^2 = 1$.

Intercept only: $X = \mathbf{1}_n$ (the $n \times 1$ vector of ones). Then:

$$H = \mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{1})^{-1}\mathbf{1}^\top = \mathbf{1}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}^\top = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$$

So $h_{ii} = 1/n$ for all $i$, and $\sum h_{ii} = n \cdot (1/n) = 1 = p + 1$ (since $p = 0$). ✓

Problem 6: MSE Decomposition of $\hat\beta$

For any estimator $\tilde\beta$ of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, the Mean Squared Error is $\text{MSE}(\tilde\beta) = E(\|\tilde\beta - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2)$. We will show the bias-variance decomposition.

Part (a) — 4 points
Define $\text{Bias}(\tilde\beta) = E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}$. By adding and subtracting $E(\tilde\beta)$, show that: $$\text{MSE}(\tilde\beta) = \|\text{Bias}(\tilde\beta)\|^2 + E(\|\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)\|^2)$$ Hint: Expand $\|\tilde\beta - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 = \|(\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)) + (E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta})\|^2$ and show the cross term vanishes.
$$\|\tilde\beta - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 = \|(\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)) + (E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta})\|^2$$ $$= \|\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)\|^2 + 2(\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta))^\top(E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}) + \|E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2$$

Taking expectations: $E(\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)) = \mathbf{0}$, so the cross term vanishes:

$$E[2(\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta))^\top(E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta})] = 2\,E(\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta))^\top(E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 2\,\mathbf{0}^\top(E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 0$$

And $E(\|E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2) = \|E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 = \|\text{Bias}\|^2$ since the bias is a fixed vector.

$$\text{MSE}(\tilde\beta) = \|\text{Bias}(\tilde\beta)\|^2 + E(\|\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)\|^2)$$
Part (b) — 3 points
Identify the two terms in the decomposition. What does $E(\|\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)\|^2)$ equal in terms of a familiar quantity?

The decomposition is: MSE = Bias² + Variance

$\|\text{Bias}(\tilde\beta)\|^2 = \|E(\tilde\beta) - \boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2$ is the squared bias.

$E(\|\tilde\beta - E(\tilde\beta)\|^2) = \text{tr}(\text{Var}(\tilde\beta))$ is the total variance (sum of variances of all components).

Part (c) — 4 points
For the OLS estimator $\hat\beta$ under the weaker linear model, what is $\text{Bias}(\hat\beta)$? Simplify the MSE in this case.

We showed in Problem 1 that $E(\hat\beta) = \boldsymbol{\beta}$, so $\text{Bias}(\hat\beta) = \mathbf{0}$.

$$\text{MSE}(\hat\beta) = \|\mathbf{0}\|^2 + \text{tr}(\text{Var}(\hat\beta)) = \text{tr}(\sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^\top X)^{-1}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2\,\text{tr}((X^\top X)^{-1})$$

For unbiased estimators, MSE = Variance. This is why Gauss-Markov says OLS minimizes variance among linear unbiased estimators — equivalently, it minimizes MSE in this class.

Quick-Reference Cheat Sheet

The Linear Model (L1–4)

Weaker Linear Model

$$\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \quad E(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \mathbf{0}, \quad \text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 I$$

Stronger Linear Model

$$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \text{MVN}(\mathbf{0},\; \sigma_\varepsilon^2 I)$$

$X$: $n \times (p{+}1)$ design matrix, first column $= \mathbf{1}$.

  • Weaker gives us: unbiased $\hat\beta$, $\text{Var}(\hat\beta)$, Gauss-Markov (BLUE)
  • Stronger adds: hypothesis tests, CIs, partial F, prediction intervals

Random Vector Properties

$E(A\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{b}) = AE(\mathbf{Z}) + \mathbf{b}$,   $\text{Var}(A\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{b}) = A\,\text{Var}(\mathbf{Z})\,A^\top$

$\text{Var}(\mathbf{Z}) = E[\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^\top] - E[\mathbf{Z}]\,E[\mathbf{Z}]^\top$

OLS Estimation (L2–4)

$$\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y} = \arg\min_{\tilde\beta}\|\mathbf{y} - X\tilde\beta\|^2$$

Linear in $\mathbf{y}$: $\hat\beta = A\mathbf{y}$,   $A = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$.

Under Weaker Model

$E(\hat\beta) = \boldsymbol{\beta}$ (unbiased),   $\text{Var}(\hat\beta) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$

$\text{Var}(\hat\beta_j) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2[(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{(j+1),(j+1)}$

Under Stronger Model

$\hat\beta \sim \text{MVN}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\; \sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^\top X)^{-1})$

$\hat\beta_j \sim N(\beta_j,\; \sigma_\varepsilon^2[(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{(j+1),(j+1)})$

Gauss-Markov Theorem (BLUE)

Under the weaker model, OLS has the smallest MSE among all linear unbiased estimators. Key: any linear $\tilde\beta = A\mathbf{y}$ with $A = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D$, $DX = \mathbf{0}$ $\Rightarrow$ $\text{Var}(\tilde\beta) = \text{Var}(\hat\beta) + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 DD^\top$.

Hat Matrix & Projections (L4, 10)

$$H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top, \quad \hat{\mathbf{y}} = H\mathbf{y}, \quad \mathbf{e} = (I - H)\mathbf{y}$$

Key Matrix Identities

  • $H^\top = H$, $H^2 = H$ (symmetric, idempotent)
  • $(I{-}H)^\top = I{-}H$, $(I{-}H)^2 = I{-}H$
  • $H(I{-}H) = \mathbf{0}$,   $X^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$
  • $H$ projects onto $\text{col}(X)$; $(I{-}H)$ projects onto $\text{col}(X)^\perp$

Diagonal Elements

$1/n \le h_{ii} \le 1$,   $\sum h_{ii} = p{+}1$,   $\hat{y}_i = h_{ii}y_i + \sum_{j \ne i} h_{ij}y_j$

Under Weaker Model

$E(\mathbf{e}) = \mathbf{0}$,   $E(\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = X\boldsymbol{\beta}$

$\text{Var}(\mathbf{e}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(I{-}H)$,   $\text{Var}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 H$

$\text{Var}(e_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1{-}h_{ii})$,   $\text{Var}(\hat{y}_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 h_{ii}$

$\text{Cov}(e_i, \hat{y}_j) = 0$ for all $i,j$,   $\text{Cov}(e_i, e_j) = -\sigma_\varepsilon^2 h_{ij}$

Under Stronger Model

$\mathbf{e} \perp\!\!\!\perp \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ (independent).   $e_i \sim N(0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2(1{-}h_{ii}))$.   If $h_{ii} = 1$: $\hat{y}_i = y_i$, $e_i = 0$.

Algebraic (always hold with intercept)

$\bar{e} = 0$,   $\text{cov}(e, \hat{y}) = 0$,   $\text{cov}(e, x_j) = 0$

$R^2$ and Sums of Squares (L4)

$\text{TSS} = \sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2$,   $\text{RSS} = \sum e_i^2$,   $\text{SSreg} = \sum(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2$

$\text{TSS} = \text{SSreg} + \text{RSS}$ (with intercept)

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}}{\text{TSS}} = \text{cor}(\hat{y}, y)^2$$

Simple regression: $R^2 = \text{cor}(x, y)^2$. $R^2$ never decreases when adding predictors.

Estimating $\sigma_\varepsilon$ (L5)

$$\hat\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - p - 1} \qquad \text{se}(\hat\beta_j) = \hat\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{[(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{(j+1),(j+1)}}$$

df $= n - p - 1$ ($p{+}1$ constraints from $\mathbf{e}^\top X = \mathbf{0}$).

Hypothesis Tests (L5–6)

t-test for $\beta_j$

$H_0: \beta_j = c$.

$$t = \frac{\hat\beta_j - c}{\text{se}(\hat\beta_j)} \sim t_{n-p-1} \quad \text{under } H_0$$

$H_a: \ne c$: $p = 2P(T \ge |t|)$.   $> c$: $P(T \ge t)$.   $< c$: $P(T \le t)$. Reject if $p \le \alpha$.

CI for $\beta_j$

$$\hat\beta_j \;\pm\; t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-p-1} \cdot \text{se}(\hat\beta_j)$$

Duality: reject $H_0: \beta_j = c$ at level $\alpha$ $\iff$ $c \notin$ the $100(1{-}\alpha)\%$ CI.

Overall F-test

$H_0: \beta_1 = \cdots = \beta_p = 0$,   $H_a$: at least one $\ne 0$.

$$F = \frac{R^2 / p}{(1 - R^2)/(n - p - 1)} = \frac{(\text{TSS} - \text{RSS})/p}{\text{RSS}/(n - p - 1)} \sim F_{p,\,n-p-1}$$

Always right-tail. $p$-value $= P(F_{p,n-p-1} \ge F_{\text{obs}})$.

Partial F-test

Full vs. reduced (nested). $H_0$: subset of $\beta$'s $= 0$.

$$F = \frac{(\text{RSS}_R - \text{RSS}_F)/(df_R - df_F)}{\text{RSS}_F / df_F} \sim F_{df_R - df_F,\;df_F}$$

Num. df $= p_F - p_R$. Overall F: reduced $=$ intercept only.

Key: $t^2_{df} = F_{1,df}$ — partial F for one predictor $=$ t-test.

Categorical Variables & Interactions (L7–8)

$G$ levels $\to$ $G{-}1$ dummy variables. Omitted $=$ reference.

$\hat\beta_j$ = est. diff. in $E(y)$ between category $j$ and reference, holding other vars fixed. Why not $G$ dummies? $\sum$ dummies $= \mathbf{1}$ $\Rightarrow$ $X^\top X$ singular.

One categorical + overall F = ANOVA: $H_0: \mu_1 = \cdots = \mu_G$.

Interactions

Cat × Cont: $x_i \cdot \mathbf{1}\{z = c\}$ gives group-specific slopes. Full interactions $=$ separate regressions per group.

Cont × Cont: include $x_1 x_2$ as predictor:

$$\frac{\partial\hat{y}}{\partial x_1} = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_{12}\,x_2$$

Simpson's Paradox: Aggregated trend reverses within subgroups when a confounder is ignored.

Linear Combinations (L8)

$$E(\mathbf{a}^\top\hat\beta) = \mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} \qquad \text{Var}(\mathbf{a}^\top\hat\beta) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2\,\mathbf{a}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\mathbf{a}$$
$$\text{se}(\mathbf{a}^\top\hat\beta) = \hat\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{\mathbf{a}^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\mathbf{a}}$$

Cannot compute from summary() alone — need $\hat\sigma_\varepsilon$ and design matrix $X$.

Prediction Error Decomposition

$$E\!\left[(y^* - \tilde{x}^\top\hat\beta)^2\right] = \underbrace{\text{Var}(\varepsilon^*)}_{\text{irreducible noise}} + \underbrace{\text{Var}(\tilde{x}^\top\hat\beta)}_{\text{estimation error}}$$

Proof: add/subtract $\tilde{x}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}$; cross term $= 0$ by independence of $y^*$ and $\hat\beta$ plus $E(\varepsilon^*) = 0$ and $E(\hat\beta) = \boldsymbol{\beta}$.
